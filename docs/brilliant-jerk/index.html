Why We Don't Hire Brilliant Jerks in the AI Era

By Rifni • Engineering Management

I have managed engineering teams long enough to see how one toxic superstar can slowly ruin a great group of people.

We all know the "brilliant jerk." It is the engineer who ships fast and solves impossible bugs, yet leaves a trail of bruised egos, snarky pull request comments, and silent team meetings behind them. For years, tech companies let this slide because finding someone who could code at that level felt impossible. You just swallowed your pride and kept them around because the business needed their output.

I saw this firsthand with another engineering manager in my department. They had one of these brilliant jerks on their squad. Whenever this person was in a meeting, the rest of the room went completely quiet. Nobody spoke up or shared ideas because this engineer was relentless at bulldozing everyone else to push their own approach—even when their ideas weren't actually the best ones. The team just gave up trying. A few months after that engineer finally left the company, the change was night and day. Suddenly, quiet engineers started speaking up in meetings, pitching ideas, and taking real ownership of the codebase. The talent was always there; it was just being suffocated.

Netflix famously put their foot down against this years ago in their culture memo, stating simply: no brilliant jerks. The cost to teamwork is just too high.

Today, in the age of AI, that rule isn't just good advice—it is common sense.

Here is the reality of modern engineering: raw coding output is no longer rare. With AI tools, a solid, collaborative mid-level engineer can now ship code, write tests, and debug systems at the speed that used to require a lone-wolf genius. We don't need to put up with bad attitudes just to hit our sprint goals anymore.

When AI handles the heavy lifting of writing boilerplate and solving routine problems, the hardest part of our job becomes human alignment. It is about sharing context, debating architecture without ego, and helping each other when things break in production. A brilliant jerk destroys that environment. People stop asking questions in Slack because they don't want to feel stupid, and good engineers quietly update their resumes and leave.

You don't have to choose between smart people and nice people. There are plenty of happy, humble, and deeply talented engineers out there who love building things with others.

When I hire today, I am not looking for a hero who wants to carry the team on their back while silencing everyone else in the room. I am looking for someone who makes the engineers sitting next to them better. AI can write the code, so my top priority is hiring humans who actually enjoy working together.
